# Chapter 4 — Galerkin Finite Element Formulation

---

# 1. Introduction

The weak formulation developed in Chapter 2 provides the continuous variational statement of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations.

The finite element method converts this infinite-dimensional problem into a finite-dimensional algebraic system by introducing approximation spaces.

The standard Galerkin method assumes that:

- the solution is approximated in a finite-dimensional space,
- the test functions belong to the same space.

This is known as the **Galerkin principle**.

The Residual-Based Variational Multiscale (RBVMS) method is constructed by extending this formulation to include unresolved scales.

---

# 2. Finite Element Approximation

The velocity and pressure spaces are approximated by finite-dimensional spaces:

$$\mathbf{u}_h\in V_h$$

and

$$p_h\in Q_h$$

The velocity field is represented using finite element basis functions:

$$\mathbf{u}_h(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{A=1}^{N_u}N_A(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{U}_A(t)$$

where:

- $N_A$ are shape functions,
- $\mathbf{U}_A$ are nodal velocity unknowns,
- $N_u$ is the number of velocity degrees of freedom.

The pressure approximation is:

$$p_h(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{B=1}^{N_p}M_B(\mathbf{x})P_B(t)$$

where:

- $M_B$ are pressure shape functions,
- $P_B$ are pressure unknowns.

---

# 3. Galerkin Approximation

The Galerkin method chooses test functions from the same finite element spaces:

$$\mathbf{v}_h\in V_h$$

and:

$$q_h\in Q_h$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\text{Trial space}=\text{Test space}}$$

This is the defining characteristic of the Galerkin method.

---

# 4. Weak Form Substitution

The continuous weak form is:

$$\rho(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t},\mathbf{v})+c(\mathbf{u};\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})+a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})-b(\mathbf{v},p)+b(\mathbf{u},q)=F(\mathbf{v})$$

The Galerkin approximation replaces:

$$\mathbf{u}\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_h$$

and:

$$p\rightarrow p_h$$

giving:

$$\rho(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_h}{\partial t},\mathbf{v}_h)+c(\mathbf{u}_h;\mathbf{u}_h,\mathbf{v}_h)+a(\mathbf{u}_h,\mathbf{v}_h)-b(\mathbf{v}_h,p_h)+b(\mathbf{u}_h,q_h)=F(\mathbf{v}_h)$$

---

# 5. Matrix Representation

After substituting the finite element expansions and assembling over all elements, the semi-discrete system becomes:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{M}\dot{\mathbf{U}}+\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{U})\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{F}}$$

where:

- $\mathbf{M}$ is the mass matrix,
- $\mathbf{N}$ represents nonlinear convection,
- $\mathbf{K}$ is the viscous stiffness matrix,
- $\mathbf{G}$ is the pressure gradient operator,
- $\mathbf{F}$ is the force vector.

The incompressibility constraint becomes:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}=0}$$

where $\mathbf{D}$ is the discrete divergence operator.

---

# 6. Saddle-Point Structure

Combining momentum and continuity equations gives:

$$\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{M}&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{U}}\\\dot{\mathbf{P}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{U})&\mathbf{G}\\\mathbf{D}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{U}\\\mathbf{P}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{F}\\0\end{bmatrix}}$$

The zero block associated with pressure indicates that pressure has no time derivative.

This creates a saddle-point problem.

---

# 7. Nonlinear Residual Form

The discrete Navier–Stokes equations can be written as:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{P})=0}$$

where:

$$\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{P})=\mathbf{M}\dot{\mathbf{U}}+\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{U})\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{K}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{F}$$

and:

$$\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}=0$$

The residual is the central quantity in RBVMS.

---

# 8. Stability Problem of Standard Galerkin FEM

The classical Galerkin formulation works well for diffusion-dominated problems.

However, for convection-dominated flows:

$$\mathrm{Pe}=\frac{UL}{\nu}\gg1$$

where:

- $U$ is characteristic velocity,
- $L$ is characteristic length,
- $\nu$ is kinematic viscosity,

the formulation becomes unstable.

Typical symptoms include:

- spurious oscillations,
- nonphysical overshoots,
- poor convergence.

---

# 9. Origin of Numerical Instability

The convection operator:

$$ (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} $$

is non-self-adjoint.

The Galerkin method does not introduce any numerical dissipation aligned with the transport direction.

Therefore, unresolved scales generated by convection are not properly represented.

The missing information appears as:

$$\boxed{\text{Resolved scales interact with unresolved scales}}$$

---

# 10. Motivation for Stabilization

Several stabilization techniques have been developed:

## Streamline Upwind Petrov–Galerkin (SUPG)

Adds stabilization in the streamline direction.

## Pressure Stabilizing Petrov–Galerkin (PSPG)

Provides pressure stabilization for incompressible flow.

## Variational Multiscale Method (VMS)

Models the effect of unresolved scales.

The VMS approach introduces the decomposition:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_h+\mathbf{u}'}$$

where:

- $\mathbf{u}_h$ represents resolved scales,
- $\mathbf{u}'$ represents unresolved scales.

---

# 11. Transition to RBVMS

The standard Galerkin formulation considers only:

$$V_h$$

However, the complete function space contains:

$$\boxed{V=V_h\oplus V'}$$

The unresolved space $V'$ contains scales smaller than the mesh resolution.

RBVMS derives a model for these unresolved scales using the residual of the resolved solution.

The fine-scale approximation becomes:

$$\boxed{\mathbf{u}'\approx-\boldsymbol{\tau}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u}_h)}$$

where:

- $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is the stabilization tensor,
- $\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{u}_h)$ is the resolved-scale residual.

---

# 12. Summary

The Galerkin finite element method provides the foundation for incompressible flow simulation.

The main steps are:

1. Approximate velocity and pressure using finite element spaces.
2. Substitute into the weak formulation.
3. Assemble the resulting algebraic system.
4. Solve the nonlinear saddle-point problem.

However, standard Galerkin FEM lacks stability for convection-dominated and turbulent flows.

This motivates the Variational Multiscale approach.

The next chapter introduces the mathematical decomposition of scales and derives the Variational Multiscale formulation.
