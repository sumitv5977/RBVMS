# Chapter 5 — Variational Multiscale (VMS) Decomposition

---

# 1. Introduction

The classical Galerkin finite element method assumes that the numerical approximation contains all physically relevant scales.

However, turbulent and convection-dominated flows contain a wide range of spatial and temporal scales.

For example, in turbulent flow:

- large scales contain most of the kinetic energy,
- intermediate scales transfer energy,
- small scales dissipate energy.

A finite element mesh cannot resolve all scales.

The Variational Multiscale (VMS) method addresses this issue by decomposing the solution into:

1. Resolved scales
2. Unresolved scales

The unresolved scales are not ignored; instead, their influence on the resolved scales is modeled.

---

# 2. General Variational Problem

Consider a nonlinear boundary value problem written abstractly as:

$$\mathcal{L}(u)=f$$

where:

- $\mathcal{L}$ is a differential operator,
- $u$ is the unknown solution,
- $f$ is the forcing term.

The weak form is:

$$B(u,v)=F(v)$$

for all admissible test functions $v$.

Here:

- $B$ represents the weak operator,
- $F$ represents the forcing functional.

---

# 3. Decomposition of Function Spaces

The fundamental assumption of VMS is that the complete solution space can be decomposed as:

$$\boxed{V=V_h\oplus V'}$$

where:

- $V_h$ is the resolved finite-dimensional space,
- $V'$ is the unresolved fine-scale space.

The symbol $\oplus$ indicates a direct sum decomposition.

Therefore, every solution can be written as:

$$\boxed{u=u_h+u'}$$

where:

- $u_h$ is the coarse-scale component,
- $u'$ is the fine-scale component.

---

# 4. Test Function Decomposition

The test function space is decomposed similarly:

$$\boxed{V=V_h\oplus V'}$$

Therefore:

$$v=v_h+v'$$

where:

- $v_h$ is the coarse-scale test function,
- $v'$ is the fine-scale test function.

---

# 5. Substitution into the Weak Form

The original variational problem is:

$$B(u,v)=F(v)$$

Substituting:

$$u=u_h+u'$$

and:

$$v=v_h+v'$$

gives:

$$B(u_h+u',v_h+v')=F(v_h+v')$$

Expanding:

$$B(u_h,v_h)+B(u_h,v')+B(u',v_h)+B(u',v')=F(v_h)+F(v')$$

This equation contains interactions between resolved and unresolved scales.

---

# 6. Coarse-Scale and Fine-Scale Problems

The VMS method separates the problem into two coupled equations.

## Coarse-scale equation

Choose:

$$v=v_h$$

giving:

$$\boxed{B(u_h+u',v_h)=F(v_h)}$$

This equation governs the resolved scales.

---

## Fine-scale equation

Choose:

$$v=v'$$

giving:

$$\boxed{B(u_h+u',v')=F(v')}$$

This equation governs the unresolved scales.

---

# 7. Interpretation of Fine Scales

The fine-scale solution satisfies a problem driven by the error in the coarse-scale approximation.

The exact solution satisfies:

$$\mathcal{L}(u)=f$$

The resolved solution satisfies approximately:

$$\mathcal{L}(u_h)\neq f$$

Therefore, the residual is:

$$\boxed{R(u_h)=f-\mathcal{L}(u_h)}$$

The unresolved scales are generated by this residual.

---

# 8. Fine-Scale Equation

The fine-scale problem can be written as:

$$\mathcal{L}(u_h+u')-f=0$$

Expanding:

$$\mathcal{L}(u_h)+\mathcal{L}(u')-f=0$$

Therefore:

$$\mathcal{L}(u')=f-\mathcal{L}(u_h)$$

or:

$$\boxed{\mathcal{L}(u')=R(u_h)}$$

The fine scales are therefore determined by the residual of the resolved scales.

---

# 9. Green's Function Representation

The exact solution of the fine-scale problem can be formally written using the Green's function:

$$u'(x)=\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)R(u_h(y))dy$$

where:

- $G(x,y)$ is the Green's function,
- $R(u_h)$ is the coarse-scale residual.

This expression shows that the unresolved scales depend on the residual throughout the domain.

---

# 10. Localization Assumption

The exact Green's function is generally too expensive to compute.

VMS introduces a localization approximation:

$$G(x,y)\approx\tau\delta(x-y)$$

where:

- $\tau$ is the stabilization operator,
- $\delta$ is the Dirac delta function.

Substituting gives:

$$\boxed{u'\approx\tau R(u_h)}$$

The unresolved scales are therefore modeled locally.

---

# 11. Residual-Based Approximation

For Navier–Stokes equations, the sign convention is commonly written as:

$$\boxed{u'\approx-\tau R(u_h)}$$

where:

- $\tau$ is the stabilization tensor,
- $R(u_h)$ is the strong residual.

This equation is the central approximation of Residual-Based VMS.

---

# 12. Physical Interpretation

The VMS decomposition can be interpreted as:

## Resolved scales

Represented by:

$$u_h$$

contain structures larger than the mesh scale.

## Unresolved scales

Represented by:

$$u'$$

contain structures smaller than the mesh resolution.

The unresolved scales influence the resolved scales through their feedback:

$$\boxed{\text{Resolved scales}\rightarrow\text{Fine scales}\rightarrow\text{Resolved scales}}$$

---

# 13. Application to Turbulent Flow

In turbulent flows, the separation of scales is essential.

The mesh resolves only part of the turbulence spectrum.

The unresolved scales represent:

- small turbulent structures,
- subgrid fluctuations,
- dissipative scales.

Unlike traditional turbulence models, VMS does not prescribe an artificial eddy viscosity directly.

Instead, it models the interaction between scales.

---

# 14. Transition to Residual-Based VMS

The VMS formulation introduces the unresolved scale equation.

However, the fine-scale problem is still too expensive to solve directly.

The next step is to approximate:

$$u'$$

using the residual:

$$\boxed{u'\approx-\tau R(u_h)}$$

The derivation of the stabilization tensor $\tau$ is therefore the next fundamental step.

---

# Summary

The Variational Multiscale method is based on:

1. Decomposition of the solution space:

$$\boxed{V=V_h\oplus V'}$$

2. Decomposition of the solution:

$$\boxed{u=u_h+u'}$$

3. Fine scales driven by residual:

$$\boxed{\mathcal{L}(u')=R(u_h)}$$

4. Local residual approximation:

$$\boxed{u'\approx-\tau R(u_h)}$$

This provides the theoretical foundation for Residual-Based Variational Multiscale stabilization.
